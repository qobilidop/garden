# A forest of evergreen notes

Griffiths uses Jon Sterling's Forester-built mathematical note forest as a
prompt for design questions rather than as a system review. The post is a
short, enthusiastic practitioner reflection: it notices how Forester names
and composes notes, then uses that example to unsettle Griffiths's earlier
distinction between a private working method and a public publication. Its
contribution is a small but useful taxonomy, not evidence that Forester
improves research or writing.

## Atomic notes leave several choices open

Forester gives each note a permanent identifier made from an author's
three-letter initials and four base-36 digits. The four-digit suffix supplies
1,679,616 possible identifiers per prefix. Griffiths likes the scheme because
it is compact and ample, but the more important point is what the identifier
does *not* decide: note hierarchy, authorship, visibility, and output format
remain separate questions. He flags hierarchy and atomicity as attractive
parts of the design without claiming that one requires the other.

The post also separates two meanings of *evergreen*. A note's **content** may
remain applicable across contexts, while its **format** may make the same unit
easy to remix into papers, lectures, presentations, and other artifacts. The
first is a claim about durability; the second is a claim about composability.
Conflating them makes a timeless essay and a reusable research fragment look
like the same design achievement.

## A working system can also be a publication

Griffiths proposes two axes for classifying note systems: single- versus
multi-author, and private versus public. Luhmann's historical slip box occupies
the private, single-author quadrant; Andy Matuschak's notes are public and
single-author; Sterling's forest is public and multi-author. Griffiths leaves
the private, multi-author quadrant as an open question rather than forcing an
example.

Those axes overturn his prior functional binary. He had treated a
Zettelkasten as a backstage process for producing artifacts and a wiki as a
public-facing artifact in its own right. Sterling's site appears to do both:
the working material remains generative infrastructure while the open studio
is itself something readers can use. The distinction therefore lies neither
in software nor visibility alone; method and publication are roles that one
system can combine.

## Assessment

- **Durable:** separating durable content from composable format, and
  separating working method from publishing posture. The two-axis
  public/private × single/multi-author frame is a useful first diagnostic
  because it makes design choices explicit instead of treating "wiki" and
  "Zettelkasten" as mutually exclusive technologies.
- **Era-bound:** the particular Forester sites and links are a June 2024
  snapshot of evolving public note systems. The specific tools may change
  while the distinctions survive.
- **Caveats:** the post is observation and conjecture. It does not test
  Forester's workflow, substantiate the suggested journalistic ancestry of
  "evergreen," or support its broad claim about twentieth-century
  Zettelkästen with a survey. Griffiths openly marks several points as
  questions or personal definitions.
- **Stake:** Griffiths writes as an advocate of atomic notes and describes his
  response as enthusiastic. That declared affinity makes the piece perceptive
  about attractive design details but deliberately non-adversarial.
- **In this library:** [[luhmann1981-slip-boxes]] explains how fixed addresses
  and links make a private note network capable of surprise;
  [[karpathy2026-llm-wiki]] makes compilation and maintenance the core of an
  agent-managed wiki; and [[matuschak2019-tools-thought]] asks whether a
  representation enables new thought. Griffiths adds the orthogonal question
  those accounts leave mostly implicit: whether the working substrate is also
  a public artifact, and whether many authors can inhabit it.
