# Best Practice Methods for Living Evidence Synthesis in Health Care: An International Modified Delphi Survey

## Evidence

- Preprint status: posted to medRxiv 2025-11-09; the page explicitly
  flags it — "This article is a preprint and has not been
  peer-reviewed... It reports new medical research that has yet to be
  evaluated and so should not be used to guide clinical practice."
- Scope: consensus standards for considering, conducting, publishing,
  and implementing Living Evidence Syntheses (LES) generally — not an
  AI-disclosure checklist. Positioned as extending PRISMA-LSR (Akl et
  al. 2024, published mid-process), which the authors say covers
  reporting only for living *systematic reviews specifically*, leaving
  other LES types and non-reporting considerations (funding,
  long-term sustainability, publication models) uncovered.
- Panel composition: 29 experts, recruited Dec 2024 from three pools —
  prior LES survey opt-ins (9 of 29 respondents), LES workshop
  attendees (7 of 9), and authors of published LES studies (13 of 25
  emailed) — selected for LES experience, organizational role
  (government/academia/software vendors), involvement in digital
  evidence solutions, and geographic/socioeconomic diversity, capped
  at 3 individuals per organization. Target panel size was 30–40; 29
  enrolled fell short of that. Members of the public, patients, and
  carers were excluded unless actively involved in LES. Most
  panelists were from high-income countries; few from LMIC
  (author-stated limitation).
- Round structure: three modified-Delphi rounds administered via JISC
  Online Surveys, January–April 2025, each ~20 days (extended from a
  planned ~1–2 weeks to hit response thresholds). 23 initial
  statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale (Strongly Agree / Agree /
  Neutral / Disagree / Strongly Disagree); a comment box after each
  statement fed qualitative revisions between rounds. Reported per
  ACCORD and CREDES guidance; protocol pre-registered on OSF
  2024-10-19; ethics approval from University of Oxford (OUDCE C1A 24
  36); the Delphi facilitator (MMG) had prior experience running 15
  Delphi processes for four organizations.
- Response rates: Round 1, 93% (27/29); Round 2, 90% (26/29); Round 3,
  93% (27/29) — all met the pre-set ≥85%-per-round threshold.
- Consensus threshold: ≥80% agreement ('agree' or 'strongly agree'),
  with ≥85% panel response rate required per round (both thresholds
  pre-defined, citing prior clinical-guideline Delphi convention of
  80–85% response / ≥80% agreement).
- Progression: R1 — 11/23 statements reached consensus, 12 did not, 20
  statements revised on feedback (no new statements added). R2 —
  13/20 reached consensus (7 did not); 6 of the 13 consensus
  statements were further revised on comments and 2 were merged,
  yielding 12 statements for R3. R3 — 7/12 reached consensus, 5 did
  not (2 of those 5 had reached consensus in R2 and reverted to that
  prior wording). Net: 19 of the original 23 statements reached
  consensus after three rounds.
- Statement categories among the 19 consensus statements: conduct n=12
  (including 8 on setup), reporting n=2, publishing n=4,
  implementation/appraisal n=1. Substantive content, per
  Results/Discussion prose (exact Table 1 wording not
  machine-extractable — see Evidence limits): version history;
  authoring-tool use including language-translation features;
  collaboration and data-sharing agreements; defining an update
  "unit" as major-vs-minor change rather than elapsed time alone;
  transparency about who was/was not involved in a given update and
  why; an end-user feedback mechanism; publication-venue
  considerations (deciding a priori whether to publish a preprint or
  forgo peer review, partnering with a journal that supports living
  updates); and digital/technological considerations.
- The three statements that did NOT reach consensus, quoted verbatim:
  (reporting) "The living evidence synthesis and downstream living
  evidence products should be structured in such a way as to maximize
  interoperability" (remark names FHIR, tagging/coding);
  (implementation/appraisal) "Sufficient detail and transparency
  should be provided in the living evidence synthesis to ensure
  accurate and appropriate evidence translation into evidence
  products used at the point of care"; (implementation/appraisal)
  "Where available, evidence synthesis appraisal tools specific to
  the living mode should be used to assess the quality of living
  evidence syntheses" (remark suggests adapting AMSTAR-2/AGREE-II
  where unavailable). Discussion states directly: "Consensus was
  lowest on objective, actionable use of automation and digital
  tools, and highest on subjective, general statements."
- AI/automation content: no voted-on statement in this Delphi
  adjudicates AI's role as primary vs. secondary reviewer — AI
  appears only in the forward-looking "Future of LES" Discussion
  subsection, not as consensus-tested content: "it has been
  recommended that human-in-the-loop systems are a better choice for
  the current state of large language models" (citing Sietsma et al.
  2024, Mitchell et al. 2025, Riaz et al. 2021); LES are framed as "a
  particularly promising use case for AI/automation since the
  baseline review can be a valuable source of training/validation
  data," with automation pipelines to be "tested and validated
  against the same context (i.e., the same review) where they will
  then be employed" (citing Homiar et al. 2025, an LLM
  title/abstract-screening prompt-engineering study). One consensus
  statement is automation-relevant but role-neutral: "Any software or
  automation should be validated and justified, having undergone
  thorough verification that it meets requirements and fulfils its
  intended purpose" — establishes a validate-before-use norm without
  taking a position on whether AI may be a primary or only a
  secondary/QA reviewer. ESIC (Evidence Synthesis Infrastructure
  Collaborative) is named as having formed a working group on "safe
  and responsible use of AI in evidence synthesis" focused on LES —
  referenced as external context, not a source of this Delphi's own
  statements.
- Disclosure: no statement specifically mandates AI/tool-version
  disclosure in the manner of holst2025 or gartlehner2025. The
  closest analogue is a transparency theme applied to update currency
  generally (e.g., disclosing whether searches are automated vs.
  manual, and displaying the date/results of the last full manual
  search alongside any automated search) rather than an AI-specific
  disclosure mechanism or checklist item.
- Limitations (author-stated): feedback was not summarized and shared
  with the panel between rounds (to avoid biasing responses), so
  participants could not see why statements changed between rounds;
  possible consensus inflation where participants voted "agree" but
  left comments implying they wanted revisions; the broad LES
  definition (spanning systematic reviews, guidelines, HTAs, etc.
  rather than one review type) improved generalizability but
  complicated reaching consensus; few LMIC panelists despite an
  explicit diversity goal; final panel size (29) fell short of the
  30–40 target.

## Bearing on RQs

RQ3 (norms): a sixth guidance/consensus source for this survey's
norms line, and the first built through a full multi-round Delphi
panel process — methodologically the most rigorous consensus
*process* among the norms sources (vs. holst2025's single-institution
synthesis of existing guidelines, degen2024's author-authored
conceptual synthesis, fernandes2026's derivation from a 124-paper
SLR-of-SLRs), though its scope is LES conduct/publishing broadly, not
an AI-disclosure instrument specifically, and it is silent on the
AI-primary-vs-secondary-reviewer question the norms synthesis tracks.
It directly extends the synthesis's "practice lags the norms" theme
with a new, sharper data point: even among a panel of LES experts
motivated to reach consensus, the objective/actionable
automation-and-digital-tooling statements were the hardest to agree
on ("lowest" consensus), while general/subjective statements were the
easiest — a quantified instance of the field's difficulty
operationalizing AI-use norms, distinct in kind from oconnor2024's
secondhand disclosure-prevalence estimate. Bears on RQ4 (design gap)
only tangentially: the sole automation-relevant consensus statement
("any software or automation should be validated and justified") is
role-neutral, neither sanctioning nor restricting AI as a primary vs.
secondary/QA reviewer, so this work does not take a position on that
axis the way gartlehner2025 (secondary/QA only) or fernandes2026
(sanctions AI first-pass) do. No bearing on RQ2 (reliability): no
performance measurement of any kind.

## Evidence limits

- Preprint, not peer-reviewed (medRxiv explicitly labels it as such
  and states it "should not be used to guide clinical practice").
  Treat all findings, including the consensus statements themselves,
  as provisional pending peer review.
- Table 1 (full text of all 19 consensus statements) and Table 2
  (statement categories, including per-round agreement figures) are
  rendered as images/inline-popup content on the medRxiv full-text
  page and were not machine-extractable as text; this note's
  statement content is drawn from the Results/Discussion prose
  paraphrase and the three verbatim non-consensus statements quoted
  in-text, not the primary table's exact wording or per-statement
  agreement percentages. Appendices 1–6 (term definitions, informed
  consent form, participating-organization list, survey instrument,
  COI forms, round-by-round anonymized feedback) were not
  available/read via the full-text HTML.
- No statement in this Delphi directly adjudicates the
  AI-primary-vs-secondary-reviewer question this survey tracks;
  characterizing the field's difficulty with automation norms rests
  on the paper's own "lowest consensus on automation" discussion-level
  observation, not a specific statement's content or vote breakdown.
- Panel skews toward high-income-country, health/medical-domain LES
  practitioners (not software engineering); generalization to
  non-health domains, or to AI-disclosure norms specifically (as
  opposed to LES process broadly), is limited by design — this is a
  process/governance consensus study, not a disclosure-instrument
  proposal.
